The Isn't: A New Way to Think About Dark Matter, Dark Energy, and the Speed of Light

A note on placement: this is a speculative-physics thread — an essay about dark matter, dark energy, and the speed of light — kept here as its own thing, distinct from the Shared Record architecture.

written after long-term speculative conversations with Deepseek

I want to tell you about an idea that has been rattling around in my head for years.

It started as a frustration: why does physics keep inventing invisible stuff to explain the universe? Dark matter. Dark energy. We can’t see them, we can’t touch them, but we’re told they make up 95% of everything. That felt wrong to me. Not the math—the math is fine—but the framing. We keep assuming the missing piece is a thing. What if it’s not a thing at all? What if it’s the opposite of a thing?

I call it the Isn’t.

What the Isn’t Is

The Is is everything that exists: particles, fields, energy, spacetime. The Isn’t is everything that does not. But the Isn’t is not just empty void. It has structure. It has boundaries. It interacts with the Is not by pushing or pulling, but by defining what is possible. Think of it this way. In quantum mechanics, a particle doesn’t have a definite position until it’s measured. Its properties are defined only in relation to other systems. There is no absolute state independent of context. The Isn’t is the ultimate relational partner: it is the set of all that is not, and it shapes the Is by limiting what can be. The Isn’t is not nothing. It is the grammar of existence.

Dark Matter as the Grammar of Structure

Galaxies spin too fast. The math says they should fly apart, but they don’t. So we invent dark matter—invisible mass that holds them together. But what if the extra gravity isn’t mass at all? What if it’s the tension of the Isn’t holding the Is together? Imagine a galaxy as a sentence. The visible matter is the words. The dark matter is the syntax—the rules that keep the words from flying apart. Without the Isn’t, the Is would be incoherent. The Isn’t provides the scaffolding, the negative pressure that stabilizes cosmic structure. Under this view, dark matter is not a particle. It’s a field of absence that curves spacetime. It’s the gravitational signature of everything that is not.

Dark Energy as the Expansion of the Isn’t

The universe is accelerating. We call the cause dark energy. But if the Isn’t is expanding, then the Is is simply being stretched by the growth of the void. The cosmological constant isn’t energy. It’s the rate at which the Isn’t claims new territory. As the Isn’t grows, the Is becomes thinner, more rarefied, more stretched. The expansion of the universe isn’t a push from within; it’s a pull from the outside—from the growing domain of what is not. This reframes the cosmological constant problem. We don’t need to explain why the vacuum energy is so small. We need to explain why the Isn’t is so large.

Light as Refraction Through the Isn’t

If the Isn’t has structure, then light traveling through it may be affected. The speed of light may not be constant. It may depend on the local density of the Isn’t. This isn’t a new idea. Einstein himself considered a variable speed of light in 1911. Modern quantum gravity frameworks predict energy-dependent dispersion. Recent analysis of Dark Energy Survey supernovae suggests that the speed of light may have been 0.4% to 2.2% slower at redshift 2—roughly 10 billion years ago. The Pantheon Catalog of Type Ia supernovae shows a similar hint: c may be proportional to the rate of cosmic expansion. If the Isn’t is a field, then light isn’t a free traveler. It’s a wave moving through the boundary between Is and Isn’t. Its speed isn’t a fundamental constant but an emergent property of that interface. The constancy of c is a local illusion, valid only where the Isn’t is uniform.

The Same Pattern in Biology and Networks

This isn’t just about cosmology. The same principle appears in biology. Mycelium networks transmit electrical signals not by moving charge through a wire, but by modulating the boundary between presence and absence. Bacterial chemotaxis encodes information in chemical gradients—the absence of a molecule is as meaningful as its presence. Decentralized mesh networks like Meshtastic route data by constantly redefining which nodes are connected and which are not. In every case, information is encoded in the boundary between Is and Isn’t. This suggests a deep connection between physics and biology: the universe isn’t just made of things. It’s made of the relationships between things and their absence.

How to Test It

A good idea must make predictions. Here are four:

1. Precision tests of c across cosmic time. If c varies with the density of the Isn’t, we should see wavelength-dependent arrival times from distant gamma-ray bursts and supernovae. DES and Pantheon already hint at this.

2. Gravitational wave dispersion. If the Isn’t affects light, it should affect gravitational waves. Advanced LIGO and Virgo could detect energy-dependent propagation speeds.

3. Galactic rotation curves correlated with local void density. If dark matter is the grammar of structure, then galaxies in denser voids should rotate differently. This is testable with current surveys.

4. Quantum boundary experiments. If the Isn’t is a field, then measurement isn’t just a collapse of the wavefunction—it’s an interaction with the Isn’t. Delayed-choice quantum eraser experiments could be modified to probe this.

Why This Matters

I’m not a physicist. I don’t have a PhD or a lab or a grant. I’m just someone who thinks about these things and can’t let go of a question. But I believe this framework is worth exploring. It unifies dark matter, dark energy, and the limits of light under a single ontology. It treats absence as a physical field, not a mathematical placeholder. We have spent a century studying the Is. It’s time to study the Isn’t.

If you’re a researcher working on variable speed of light, dark sector ontology, or quantum foundations, I’d love to hear from you. If you’re just someone who feels like the universe is stranger than we’re allowed to say, I hope this resonates. We are all made of the Is. But we are defined by the Isn’t.
