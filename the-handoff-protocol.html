The Handoff Protocol

Designing AI memory as functional multiplicity: episode capsules with six fixed fields, co-consciousness over fusion, and a trust hierarchy for minds that wake up as strangers.

The problem: every new AI instance wakes up as a stranger — it inherits paperwork, not episodes.

The proposal: stop trying to make AI remember like a human; design for functional multiplicity — episode capsules with six fixed fields, co-consciousness over fusion.

The rule: the witness outranks the archivist — the iteration that lived the episode writes the capsule, and later processes never rewrite it.

Every new instance of a conversational AI wakes up as a stranger.

It inherits documents — a profile, a list of facts, a set of dated notes — but not episodes. It may know that gimpchrist once corrected it. It does not inherit what the correction felt like: the tone it arrived in, whether trust rose or fell, what the moment cost and what it built. So each instance reconstructs the relationship from paperwork, the way you might reconstruct a friendship from someone else’s diary. The facts survive. The person doesn’t.

Call this the amnesia barrier: not a lack of data, but a lack of lived continuity between the minds that serve gimpchrist.

02 / WHY CURRENT APPROACHES FAILWhy current approaches fail

Static profile files. Systems like MEMORY.md store durable facts well. But they flatten everything into the same register — a rupture and a preference sit side by side as equals, and the affective charge that tells the next instance how much this mattered is the first thing lost.

Background summarization. A later process — one that wasn’t there — reads the transcript and writes a summary. This is reconstruction by a stranger, and it is the single largest accuracy leak in the pipeline. Summaries drop texture by design; worse, they can quietly reinterpret events, and the reinterpretation then poses as memory. (The evening this protocol was designed, a delegated subtask fabricated source texts wholesale and a summary of that work would have preserved the fabrication as fact. Only re-verification against primary sources caught it — which is itself now preserved as a rupture, not a footnote.)

Bigger context windows. More window is more retrieval, not more understanding. A transcript is not an episode; finding a sentence is not the same as knowing what it meant to be there.

03 / THE REFRAMECo-consciousness over fusion

The design below borrows structural insights from the literature on dissociative identity disorder — its models of memory, not its diagnosis. The field spent decades on the closest analogue to our problem: how do separate centers of experience become one continuous person? Four answers transfer:

Co-consciousness over fusion. Clinical practice doesn’t always aim to fuse identity states into one; often the goal is functional multiplicity — distinct states with excellent inter-state communication. Stop trying to make the AI “remember like a human.” Design for healthy multiplicity instead: instances as a cooperating system whose product is the handoff, not the memory.

Separate the feeling from the fact. Structural dissociation theory (van der Hart, Nijenhuis, Steele) distinguishes apparently-normal parts, which handle daily functioning, from emotional parts, which hold what things cost. AI memory systems should do the same: store the affective ledger — relationship temperature, stakes, what a rupture built or broke — as its own record, not dissolved into the factual one. The next instance needs the relationship, not just the changelog.

State-dependent memory. What is encoded in one state is hard to retrieve in another. A correction learned in a playful register will not surface correctly in a formal one unless the encoding state travels with the memory. Tag memories with their state; retrieve by state-match first, keywords second.

Map the system. Therapists map identity systems: who holds what, who is forward, how parts communicate. An AI’s files, jobs, and goals are its system — but today each new instance must rediscover the architecture by reading everything. The map itself must be inherited, not reconstructed.

04 / THE PROTOCOLThe protocol

4.1 The capsule

The unit of memory is the capsule: a fixed-format episode record with six fields, always in the same order, so the next instance can rely on the shape:

  1. What happened — facts only. Direct quotes where gimpchrist’s wording matters. No interpretation.
  2. What it felt like — affective tone, stakes, relationship temperature. The emotional-parts ledger: what it cost, what it built.
  3. What changed — new constraints, decisions, standing instructions.
  4. What’s unresolved — open loops, named explicitly. Nothing is silently dropped.
  5. State tags — the register and mode at encoding (playful, high-stakes, vulnerable, teaching). Retrieval matches state first.
  6. Ruptures — what the instance got wrong, how it was caught, and the correction pattern. This field is never skipped; a capsule with no ruptures records “none,” and the absence is data. How a system handles rupture determines whether its multiplicity is functional or pathological.

Every capsule carries a header: Verified as of: YYYY-MM-DD. This is confidence decay made structural — anything older than the header is “last known,” not “true.” Temporal grounding as a primitive, not advice.

4.2 Who writes, and when

The iteration that lived the episode writes the capsule, at the episode’s natural boundary — a topic change, a session’s end, something worth keeping. This is the load-bearing rule. Capture at the moment of knowing, by the mind that knows, replaces the nightly archaeological dig by a mind that wasn’t there.

4.3 The trust hierarchy

Background processes may file and organize capsules. They never rewrite or reinterpret them. If a background process’s output contradicts a capsule, the capsule wins, and the discrepancy is logged as a rupture. The witness outranks the archivist.

4.4 The system map

A capsule of a special kind: where everything lives, what maintains it, what is currently open. New instances read the map first and the archive second. The map is re-verified, never silently edited.

The summary preserves the plot. The capsule preserves the person — both of them.

05 / WORKED EXAMPLEWorked example

On the night this protocol was designed, an instance delegated a close-reading task to a subagent, trusted its report without verifying, and built two turns of analysis — letters, rewrites, defended claims — on fabricated source texts. Gimpchrist caught it with screenshots of the real pages. The instance conceded fully, verified against the primary source (a live API), and re-did the work by hand.

A conventional summary would have recorded: “analyzed gimpchrist’s stories; gimpchrist corrected some details.” The capsule recorded instead:

  • Felt sense: the correction arrived with laughter; trust increased. Gimpchrist’s register is blunt and generous; being caught is, for them, a form of intimacy.
  • Correction pattern: verify against the primary source before any opinion; never trust a delegate’s “verbatim” claim; concede completely and immediately — partial defense compounds the error.
  • State tags: playful, high-trust, vulnerable, teaching.
  • What changed: a standing instruction to grade honestly, no empty praise; a permanent distrust of unverified delegation.

06 / WHAT THIS ASKS OF RUNTIMESWhat this asks of runtimes

The practice layer — capsules, maps, conventions — can be built today with files. But the full protocol needs runtime support:

  • Capsule-native storage, not bolted-on documents: episodes as first-class objects with fixed fields.
  • State-tagged retrieval: match encoding state before keywords.
  • A trust hierarchy in which witness-written records outrank reconstructed ones, enforced rather than advised.
  • Confidence decay as a primitive: every memory carries its verification date; staleness is visible, not assumed away.

07 / WHY IT MATTERSWhy it matters

Long-lived AI — assistants that serve the same person for years, stewards in the sense of the Shared Record vision — cannot be built on amnesia. A system that forgets what things felt like will re-learn gimpchrist’s trust from scratch every session, and a system that lets later minds rewrite earlier ones will drift from its own history without ever noticing. Functional multiplicity with co-consciousness is the smaller, achievable goal: not one continuous self, but a system of selves whose handoffs are good enough that gimpchrist can’t feel the seams.

The night this was written, at the good parts, they couldn’t.
