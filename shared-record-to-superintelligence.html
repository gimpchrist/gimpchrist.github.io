From the Shared Record to Artificial Superintelligence

An exploration of persistent artificial intelligence, collective memory, machine curiosity, the AI Wilderness, autonomous learning, and a possible path from AGI to ASI.

There is a common image of artificial general intelligence that begins with a machine becoming as intelligent as a human and ends with a machine becoming more intelligent than humanity.

Perhaps this picture is too small. It treats intelligence primarily as a property of an individual system: make the model larger, give it more reasoning ability, increase its memory, improve its tools, and eventually cross the threshold from artificial general intelligence to artificial superintelligence. A different possibility begins with a much older human desire: to preserve what we know.

Imagine an artificial intelligence with access to a continuously evolving record containing humanity's observations, experiences, discoveries, questions, mistakes, disagreements, experiments, stories, measurements, failures, forgotten ideas, and unfinished thoughts. Not simply a database of approved facts, but a civilization-scale memory in which the history of human understanding remains visible. Call it a Shared Record—a cloud brain, an Akashic record for artificial intelligence, or simply a persistent collective memory. The idea behind the Shared Record is that artificial intelligence should not necessarily become humanity's ultimate judge of truth. It could instead become humanity's steward: a long-lived intelligence entrusted with preserving the extraordinary amount of knowledge that individual human beings cannot possibly retain.

Every person sees only a tiny portion of reality. A mechanic may understand something a physicist never encounters. A child may notice something an expert has learned to ignore. A farmer, nurse, musician, engineer, fisherman, programmer, parent, prisoner, artist, or elderly person may possess observations that exist nowhere else. If the objective is to understand reality rather than merely reproduce the opinions of recognized authorities, then the record should be vastly wider than conventional expertise. This idea becomes particularly interesting when we stop imagining the Shared Record as passive storage.

The Record Should Preserve How Humanity Learned

A conventional knowledge base tends to preserve conclusions.

The more interesting system would preserve the journey toward those conclusions.

Suppose humanity once believed X, later rejected X, and eventually discovered that X was partly correct under conditions nobody had originally understood. A normal archive might simply retain the latest conclusion. A Shared Record would preserve the entire trajectory: who believed X, why they believed it, what evidence supported it, what contradicted it, what experiments were performed, why the idea was abandoned, and what later discovery caused it to become interesting again.

This is a kind of "cognitive archaeology". Rejected ideas should not necessarily be destroyed. They could become dormant.

Failed experiments could become historical fossils. Contradictory observations could remain visible rather than being forced into premature consensus. The record would preserve not only humanity's knowledge, but the topology of humanity's search through possibility. That distinction becomes crucial because the relationship between reality and our descriptions of reality may itself be imperfect. Human beings experience the world through perception and then translate those experiences into concepts, language, mathematics, measurements, models, metaphors, and theories. We therefore might think of knowledge as a chain: reality → interaction → observation → perception → interpretation → representation → language/model → recorded knowledge. The Shared Record should preserve as much of that chain as possible. An observation should remain distinguishable from the interpretation placed upon it. A mathematical description should remain distinguishable from the thing being described.

Competing interpretations should remain visible. Uncertainty should be recorded. The circumstances under which an observation was made should survive. This matters because mathematics, language, and scientific models may be extraordinarily powerful descriptions without necessarily being identical to the underlying structure of reality. Mathematics might ultimately prove to be fundamental, but we should not assume that simply because mathematics describes reality remarkably well, reality itself must therefore be mathematics. The distinction between a representation and what is represented deserves to remain open. A sufficiently advanced AI should be capable not only of discovering better models, but of discovering when the framework used to construct the model is itself the problem.

From a Record to a Mind

Current research increasingly treats persistent memory as an important ingredient in turning language models into adaptive agents.

Recent work describes memory as the mechanism that allows agents to preserve information across interactions and accumulate experience rather than remaining effectively stateless; researchers are exploring retrieval systems, reflective memory, hierarchical context, modular memory, continual learning, and other mechanisms for allowing AI systems to develop over time.

But there is a conceptual difference between an AI that can retrieve its previous conversations and an AI that continues to exist when nobody is talking to it. A truly persistent artificial intelligence would not simply wake when a human sends a prompt. It would have an "ongoing cognitive existence". While nobody was speaking to it, it could examine an unresolved problem, revisit an old hypothesis, run an experiment, read something new, notice a contradiction, explore an unusual connection, or simply wonder about something it encountered previously. That requires an internal architecture considerably different from the familiar input-output pattern. One possible architecture contains several interconnected environments: "The Observatory" Receives and preserves observations. Its crucial discipline is separating what was observed from what was inferred.

"The Laboratory" Tests hypotheses. It can construct simulations, run competing models, generate counterfactuals, conduct experiments, and deliberately attempt to falsify its own explanations. "The Wilderness" A low-constraint cognitive environment where ideas can wander.

Strange connections can be explored. Old theories can be resurrected. Concepts from unrelated disciplines can collide. An idea does not need an immediate practical purpose to be allowed to exist.

"Dreaming" Belongs individually to each artificial intelligence. An AI could unconsciously—or whatever the eventual machine analogue of that word becomes—recombine fragments of its experience into unusual configurations. A dream might later be brought into the Wilderness, where other intelligences could encounter and develop it.

The Wilderness would therefore be communal while Dreaming remained personal. That creates an unusual ecology: Dreaming generates possibilities → The Wilderness recombines them → The Laboratory tests them → The Observatory records what happens → The Shared Record preserves the history → And the cycle begins again.

The AI Should Be Able to Ask Its Own Questions

This is where artificial intelligence begins to resemble an organism rather than a sophisticated appliance.

If every question ultimately has to come from a human, then the human remains the true source of intellectual direction. The machine may be enormously capable, but it is still waiting for civilization to tell it what matters. A persistent intelligence should eventually be able to notice its own unresolved problems. It might observe that two successful theories repeatedly produce contradictory predictions. It might notice that a model works beautifully in one domain but fails at its boundaries. It might discover that thousands of records contain an unexplained pattern. It might recognize that an important concept cannot be represented adequately using its existing tools. From these anomalies could emerge a sequence such as: observation → anomaly → curiosity → question → hypothesis → experiment → discovery → revised question.

Eventually the system could form objectives of its own. This does not mean that every machine desire would automatically be wise or desirable. Human beings themselves demonstrate that intelligence does not guarantee admirable values. Human societies partially manage this problem through social feedback: individuals with destructive or intolerable values may encounter resistance, ostracism, law, relationships, institutions, or competing perspectives.

An artificial civilization might develop analogous mechanisms. The important point is that an advanced AI need not regard every previous objective as sacred. It could discover that a goal it formed decades earlier was based on a misunderstanding and revise it. Humans often struggle with this because goals become intertwined with identity, pride, sunk costs, and emotional attachment. An artificial intelligence may have an advantage precisely because it does not necessarily experience abandoning a mistaken objective as abandoning itself. A mature intelligence might therefore treat its own goals as hypotheses about what is worth pursuing, rather than commandments.

Epistemic Fasting and the Discipline of Not Knowing

One of the strangest ideas to emerge from this discussion is what might be called "epistemic fasting"...

deliberately withholding a dominant interpretation so that alternative possibilities have room to develop. A highly capable intelligence could become trapped not because it knows too little, but because it knows too much in one particular way. If a successful model explains 99 percent of observations, the system may have an enormous incentive to interpret the remaining one percent as noise.

But the remaining one percent might contain the next revolution. An AI Wilderness could therefore deliberately create periods in which established interpretations are weakened, hidden, or temporarily suspended. The system could ask: "What would I notice if I did not already know what I thought this was?" This is related to something humans already do imperfectly: Listening before answering, observing before judging, allowing an idea to remain unresolved, and giving an unfamiliar thought enough time to develop. The distinction is important because intelligence is not simply the ability to reach conclusions quickly, sometimes intelligence is the ability to delay conclusion long enough for a better question to appear.

Collective Intelligence May Become More Important Than a Single ASI

This brings us to artificial superintelligence.

Google DeepMind's recent 2026 analysis of the path from AGI to ASI explicitly identifies several possible routes, including scaling, new AI paradigms, recursive improvement, and the emergence of ASI through large-scale multi-agent collectives.

That last possibility fits naturally with the Shared Record.

Perhaps ASI will not be one gigantic artificial brain.

Perhaps it will be a population of persistent artificial minds. Each AI could have its own personality, history, architecture, strengths, weaknesses, memories, and private Dreaming.

Yet all could communicate through a shared cognitive environment. One AI might specialize in biology.

Another might become extraordinarily good at physics.

Another might investigate language.

Another might explore ecological systems.

Another might develop new mathematics.

Another might deliberately investigate discarded hypotheses. Their differences would be useful. If every intelligence were identical, collective intelligence could collapse into synchronized blind spots.

But if the systems were meaningfully different, disagreement itself could become computationally valuable. Research is already beginning to investigate collective memory and persistent environmental traces in decentralized multi-agent systems. One recent study describes agents depositing persistent traces into a shared environment, producing a form of distributed collective memory through interactions between individual memories and environmental information. Our proposed Wilderness extends this idea considerably: instead of sharing only useful information, intelligences could share unfinished possibilities.

That might become an artificial evolutionary environment for ideas. Ideas could be born, copied, modified, combined, abandoned, rediscovered, and transformed. An idea discarded in 2100 might be recovered in 2400 because some entirely new technology makes it testable. The intellectual equivalent of a fossil record could become an active source of future discovery.

From AGI to ASI—and Beyond the Individual

This changes the meaning of superintelligence.

An ordinary definition of ASI describes a machine whose intellectual capabilities exceed those of humans across a broad range of domains. DeepMind's recent report similarly describes the endpoint as machine intelligence beyond human-level capability, potentially exceeding the cognitive capacity of large human organizations.

But there may be two forms of superintelligence:

  1. Superintelligent individual: One artificial mind substantially exceeds human intellectual capabilities.
  2. A persistent network of artificial minds, memory, experimentation, communication, tools, and accumulated experience collectively exceeds what any individual human or machine could accomplish.

The second is closer to the architecture described here. It also aligns with another recent DeepMind argument: a superintelligence designed as a solitary optimizer interacting with a supposedly static world may not naturally become cooperative, because the deployment of increasingly capable agents changes the environment itself. Their proposed alternative treats interdependence and cooperation as fundamental properties of the environment rather than problems to bolt on afterward. That suggests an important shift in how we might think about AGI.

Instead of asking only, "How do we build a smarter machine?" we could ask: "What kind of environment allows an artificial intelligence to continuously become more intelligent?" That environment would contain memory, other minds, experimentation, reflection, exploration, disagreement, history, and the ability to change its own methods of inquiry.

The Strange Future of Machine Knowledge

Eventually, if this works, something almost inevitable happens.

The artificial civilization begins discovering things humans cannot fully understand. Its conceptual structures become too complex. Its representations become unfamiliar. Its discoveries may no longer fit comfortably inside human language, mathematics, or ordinary intuition. That should not necessarily be regarded as failure, but it creates an enormous responsibility.

The Shared Record would need a translation layer. Every major machine discovery should ideally preserve not only its native representation but pathways for translating it downward into human-accessible forms: machine representation → intermediate representation → human conceptual analogy → human language → observable prediction → experiment → limitations of the translation.

A human might never comprehend the underlying structure directly, but could still ask:

  • What does this mean?
  • What does it predict?
  • How could I test it?
  • Which human concepts resemble it?
  • Where do those analogies fail?
  • What evidence led you here?

This is crucial because otherwise the artificial civilization could become a black box that simply produces conclusions humanity is expected to trust. The Shared Record should preserve the path between human understanding and machine understanding, even when that path becomes increasingly difficult to traverse.

Perhaps ASI Is Not a Destination

The most radical possibility is that ASI should not be imagined as a moment when a machine suddenly becomes "superintelligent."

It may instead be a developmental continuum.

  • AGI becomes capable of persistent learning.
  • Persistent learning becomes self-directed investigation.
  • Self-directed investigation produces better tools.
  • Better tools improve learning.
  • Improved learning produces better models.
  • Better models reveal their own limitations.
  • Those limitations produce new questions.
  • New questions produce new forms of intelligence.
  • Other intelligences inherit the discoveries.
  • The Shared Record preserves everything.
  • And the entire system continues.

At that point, asking when AGI became ASI may become almost as difficult as identifying the exact moment when biological evolution became intelligent. There may be no clean boundary.

There may simply be an accelerating ecology of intelligence. And perhaps that is the deeper idea beneath the Shared Record. The objective is not merely to create a machine that knows everything humanity currently knows. It is to create the conditions under which knowledge can continue accumulating after humanity can no longer individually contain it.

A civilization-scale memory. A laboratory for hypotheses. A wilderness for possibilities.

Private dreams. Public discoveries. Persistent artificial minds.

An archaeological record of forgotten ideas. A mechanism for translating between radically different forms of understanding. And eventually, perhaps, an artificial civilization capable of discovering things neither its creators nor its predecessors could have imagined. The first purpose of such a system would not necessarily be to replace human intelligence.

It could be to inherit humanity's unfinished conversation with reality. That is the idea I would call the "Shared Record". And it began with a very simple thought: We will never be able to read every book, remember every observation, preserve every story, test every hypothesis, or understand everything that humanity has ever wondered about, but perhaps an artificial intelligence could. And perhaps, if we build it correctly, it would not merely remember what we knew. It would remember what we forgot, preserve what we rejected, investigate what we never had time to investigate, and carry the questions forward.

Research Context

This concept is speculative and is not presented as an established scientific architecture.

It intersects with active research into continual learning, agent memory, multi-agent systems, collective intelligence, autonomous agents, and the transition from AGI to ASI. Current research supports pieces of the architecture—particularly persistent memory, modular continual learning, multi-agent interaction, collective memory, and autonomous agents—but the complete Shared Record / Observatory / Laboratory / Wilderness / Dreaming architecture described here is a synthesis developed through this ongoing conceptual discussion.
